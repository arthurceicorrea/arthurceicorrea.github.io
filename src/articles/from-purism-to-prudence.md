> *[Sapientis est ordinare.]{lang=la}* — Saint Thomas Aquinas, *Summa contra Gentiles* I, 1

Every new instrument meets two opposite errors: the enthusiasm that expects it to do our thinking, and the purism that refuses it because it makes the work easier. Artificial intelligence is meeting both. This essay argues, from Aristotle and Saint Thomas, that the question is not how hard a method is but whether it serves its end, and who remains responsible for the result.

## Means exist for an end

In the Aristotelian-Thomistic tradition, every human action is ordered to an end (*[telos]{lang=grc-Latn}*), and the means (*[ea quae sunt ad finem]{lang=la}*) are chosen for the sake of that end. Seen this way, what we might call methodological purism — treating the difficulty of a process as a measure of its worth — confuses the means with the end. The effort a method requires is not, in itself, a merit. This holds in the order of art. In the moral order, labor borne for a good end can be ascesis and, done in charity, merit before God; but the root of that merit is charity, not the clumsiness of the method.

Aristotle defines art (*[technē]{lang=grc-Latn}*) as a disposition to make things with true reason (*[logos alēthēs]{lang=grc-Latn}*), and ties the excellence of action to right reason (*[orthos logos]{lang=grc-Latn}*), as he explains in Book VI of the *Nicomachean Ethics*. A craftsman is not better for having worked harder than necessary, but for having made well what he set out to make. Saint Thomas Aquinas agrees: art is right reason about things to be made (*[recta ratio factibilium]{lang=la}*), and the craftsman, as such, is praised not for the will with which he works but for the quality of the work.

## The instrument serves the intellect

Saint Thomas, developing what Aristotle only sketched, distinguishes the principal agent from the instrument. The instrument acts by the power of the one who moves it and can produce an effect beyond its own capacity, but always under his direction. It serves, however, through an operation of its own: the saw cuts because it is sharp, and only by cutting does it make the bed the carpenter intends. The pen leaves ink; it is the writer who writes the letter, through the pen. The same holds for a calculator, a compiler or a language model. A language model has a far wider operation of its own than a pen, which is exactly why the principal agent’s direction must be more vigilant, not less. It understands nothing; what it produces signifies only for an intellect that receives and judges it.

A mathematician who has mastered arithmetic loses nothing by handing long computations to a calculator. He frees his attention for what is properly his:

- understanding the structure of the problem;
- formulating hypotheses rigorously;
- interpreting the result.

## What should not be delegated

The analogy has a limit that deserves to be stated. The mathematician can delegate arithmetic because he once learned to do it by hand. Whoever is still acquiring a skill needs the exercise itself, for skill is a habit (*[habitus]{lang=la}*), and habits are formed by repeated acts. A student who delegates what he has not yet learned does not free his intellect; he deprives it of formation.

A second limit concerns judgment. An instrument can produce a result, but responsibility for that result remains with the agent. In my own field of DevOps and infrastructure, code, configurations and pipelines suggested by AI must be read, tested and understood before they reach production. A model can write a plausible access policy in seconds; only someone who reads it knows whether it grants more than it should. Prudence includes caution (*[cautio]{lang=la}*): knowing what must be verified, and verifying it.

## Prudence, not enthusiasm or refusal

There are serious reasons for caution about AI: confident errors, security risks, questions of intellectual property, and skills that weaken when they are never practiced. These concerns deserve engagement, not dismissal. The error I am criticizing is narrower: rejecting a useful instrument on principle, as if refusing it were a virtue in itself.

Prudence (*[phronēsis]{lang=grc-Latn}*) asks for something more demanding than either enthusiasm or refusal: to judge, case by case, which means truly serve the end. Artificial intelligence is a powerful instrument, and what holds of every instrument holds of it. The work remains, as it always has, the work of the person who, by the intellect, conceives the end, directs the means and answers for the result.
